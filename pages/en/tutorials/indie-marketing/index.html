You have a side project or an idea to build. Work on what connects it to a customer: a real problem, an understandable offer and a sustainable way to reach the right people.

Nine steps in order. Each leaves you with something needed by the next: traces of a problem, conversations, an offer, a page or a distribution test. Use a SaaS, plugin, small app or paid tool. Adapt the steps to how it is used and purchased.

You need somewhere to keep notes and a project to work on. Example figures and offers are simulated. Sources distinguish empirical research, technical documentation and practical advice.

Already launched?

Use the first three steps to check your assumptions. Then go to where the journey stops: getting visitors, reaching first value, buying or returning.

01 / 09

Find a problem people already spend time or money on

You open the editor, picture an interesting feature and start building. A few evenings later, something works. One question still lives outside the repository: who has a concrete reason to use it? Your first marketing task is to find that reason before adding more features.

Look for the awkward job

Start with work you know or can observe: cleaning a CSV, chasing approvals, comparing quotes or preparing a report. Identify what triggers the task, the steps involved and the result someone needs. “An app for freelancers” is broad; “collecting client approvals before delivery” is a problem you can observe.

Look in reviews of existing tools, public support requests, relevant community discussions and the work of people you can meet. A complaint is a starting point. How someone handles the job today tells you more. A spreadsheet maintained by hand is already a competitor.

What you observeWhat to investigate
“I export three files to do this”Which files, how often, and where does the time go?
“This software costs too much”Which part do they actually use? Would they switch tools and process?
“I wish it had this feature…”When did they last miss it? What happened as a result?

Choose a direction you can investigate

Compare a few problems on frequency, consequences, the current workaround and access to the people involved. An expensive problem you cannot observe may also be hard to sell a solution for. A frequent but minor annoyance can remain an annoyance for years.

Competitors show that offers exist; they do not prove there is room for yours. No competitors may mean opportunity or weak demand. Look for a specific need that an existing solution leaves unmet.

On your project

Collect three problems and a verifiable trace for each: an incident, a discussion or a real procedure. Record who encounters it, when, how they handle it and what it costs them. Choose the direction where you can talk to real people. That becomes the input for your next step.

02 / 09

Find your first ten potential customers before writing code

You have a direction. Find out whether the problem repeats among the people you would like to sell to. Ten contacts give you a working goal. Market validation requires further evidence. You need specific conversations that let you correct your assumptions.

Start with people you can reach

Describe an initial group with a shared situation, such as small studios delivering websites to external clients. “Anyone working online” will not help you find people, choose a message or understand who decides to buy. Separate the user, the person approving the budget and anyone who must authorize data access.

Find people in relevant communities, your professional network and discussions where the problem is already visible and contact is welcome. Respect the rules and ask whether they are available. This guide does not require mass messaging: an accepted conversation is more useful than a harvested list of addresses.

Ask about the last time

Before showing your idea, ask about a recent event. Presenting the solution first can steer the conversation toward your pitch and obscure the current workflow. Y Combinator’s How to Talk to Users provides practical advice for these conversations.

A question that suggests the answerA question that gathers facts
“Would automating this help?”“How did you complete this task last time?”
“Would you pay €15?”“Which tools do you pay for today, and who chooses them?”
“Does my solution look better?”“What has stopped you switching solutions?”

Where possible and with permission, ask to see the process. Record words, steps and constraints. “It takes too long” needs a follow-up. “Every Friday I combine four exports and check duplicates by hand” gives you a concrete situation to work on. You do not need to retain confidential customer data.

Find the recurring need within a group

Look for repeated patterns within the same kind of customer. If everyone wants a different product, narrow the audience or investigate another direction. Ten requests do not require ten features: look for the shared outcome and the constraints that matter.

No response does not prove the problem is absent. Trust, context or wording may explain it. An enthusiastic yes also remains an opinion until someone acts on it.

On your project

List relevant people and conduct the conversations you can arrange. For each, record a recent incident, current solution, cost of the problem, decision maker and willingness to try something. Describe the group with the strongest recurring evidence, keeping contradictory findings visible.

03 / 09

Test the idea with a concrete offer

Conversations helped you understand the problem. Now propose an exchange: a specific result, with a visible scope and price. Find out whether interest survives a request for time, access to actual work or money.

Write an offer someone can accept

Use this structure: “I help [people in a specific situation] achieve [result], through [an understandable mechanism], with [limits and conditions].” Add the price, current product status and next step. A plugin may use a license, a small SaaS a subscription, and a new workflow a paid pilot.

Learning example · pilot offer

“For small web studios: collect delivery approvals through one link. The pilot covers one project, costs €30 and includes setup together. Export is available; integrations with other management tools are outside this version.”

The example price illustrates the structure. It is not a benchmark. The cost of the current workflow, alternatives, the value of the result and your service costs can inform a pricing hypothesis. A response to an actual offer gives you better evidence.

Choose the smallest test that answers your question

If your uncertainty is comprehension, show a demo and ask people to describe the result. If it is workflow fit, offer a trial on a real task. If it is willingness to pay, present something they can buy or a pilot with clear terms. A waiting list can help you reconnect with interested people; its size is not the same as paying demand.

You can deliver part of the work manually behind the scenes if you disclose that arrangement. This lets you test the outcome before automating everything. If you take payment for future availability, explain availability, delivery and applicable terms. Do not simulate a completed purchase or a feature that already exists.

Decide how you will interpret the test in advance

Write down the audience, offer, requested commitment, duration or number of opportunities, and decision rule. For example: “I will offer a pilot to five studios with the same problem. If none accepts, I will investigate why before building integrations.” This practical experiment rule helps organize your work; statistical inference requires an appropriate study design.

An objection about price, one about data handling and one about how often the need arises require different responses. Keep the reasons: a count of yes and no answers is insufficient.

On your project

Write a five-line offer and choose a test matching your main uncertainty. Set a rule for continuing, changing or stopping. Your next step is to make the offer understandable without explaining it in person.

04 / 09

Build a page that makes your offer understandable

Your landing page should let someone in your audience recognize the problem, understand the result and choose a next step. Start with words you heard in conversations. The technology behind the product comes after what it lets someone do.

Answer the visitor’s questions in order

The first screen should communicate the audience, outcome and action. Then show how the product works, relevant limits, answers to objections and the price or way to request a proposal. If it is still a pilot, say so next to the action.

The example is deliberately annotated: the numbers show which question each part answers. On your site, use screenshots of the actual product and an action that works. A mockup should not imply that an unavailable feature already exists.

Show evidence you actually have

A short workflow recording, sample output and explanation of limitations can help an initial evaluation. Testimonials require actual experience and permission. Until you have them, demonstrate what the product does. Use genuine logos, reviews and results only when you have permission and evidence.

Choose a primary action matching the project’s stage: try an available tool, request a demo or apply for a pilot. Put the details needed for that decision beside the action, including price, whether a card is needed and how the trial works.

Check the path before attracting traffic

Show the page to someone in your chosen audience. Ask what it offers, who it is for and what will happen after pressing the button. Then let them complete the action on a phone. Check form confirmation, email delivery, errors and accessibility: a click ending in a broken form does not measure demand.

For implementation, use the guides to HTML, accessibility and deployment. Connect organic discovery to technical SEO.

On your project

Publish a page with an audience, outcome, demonstration, terms and one primary action. Check the complete path with a person. You now have a destination for relevant traffic.

05 / 09

Bring in your first users without spreading yourself everywhere

You have an offer and a usable page. Choose where to meet people with the problem. Focus your first distribution test on one channel so you can understand the message, effort required and quality of the contacts.

Choose according to where the need appears

SituationChannel to considerA concrete first test
People search for the problem on GoogleSEO and task-focused contentPublish a useful solution to a specific search and link the product where it helps.
People discuss the problem togetherRelevant communitiesContribute to a permitted discussion and demonstrate a solution in context.
You can identify a few highly relevant peopleAgreed conversations and demonstrationsOffer a demo to people who have agreed to discuss the problem.
Your product extends an existing toolMarketplaces or partnershipsInvestigate requirements, demand, costs and visibility within the ecosystem.
You can recognize buying intent and have a test budgetTargeted advertisingTest one audience and message with a defined spending limit.

Choose among these practical options according to your project’s circumstances. A useful channel reaches your own audience at a sustainable cost in money and work. Sharing your build process may attract other developers: check whether they are also potential customers.

Treat launch as a distribution test

Before posting in a community or directory, prepare the page, demo, answers to likely questions and time to assist early users. Adapt the message to the place and its rules. A launch platform announcement may produce a spike. Check what happens in the product afterwards.

For each test, record whom you want to reach, where, with which content, for how long, at what expense and which action you want to observe. Include your hours. An article that takes time and brings customers over months behaves differently from an ad that stops distributing when spending ends.

Prepare measurement before sharing the link

A link can include parameters such as utm_source, utm_medium and utm_campaign. For example: ?utm_source=design_community&utm_medium=community&utm_campaign=pilot_01. They identify source, medium and initiative when an analytics system collects and interprets them. Keep naming consistent and do not put personal data in URLs. See Google’s campaign-link documentation.

Source information may be missing or change across devices and visits. Where helpful, add an optional question about how someone found you. Attribution is not a complete reconstruction of the customer’s path.

Before enabling analytics and pixels

Decide which events you need, which data you collect and how long you keep it. For a site serving Italy, the Italian data protection authority’s cookie and tracking guidelines distinguish technical tools from other tracking, with specific conditions for analytics treated as technical tools. “Cookieless” does not automatically remove obligations: configuration, purposes and processing matter. Check the requirements applicable to your situation before enabling tracking.

On your project

Write a plan for one channel: audience, message, destination, expected action, duration, hours and spending limit. Set up essential measurement and check that it distinguishes internal testing from actual use.

06 / 09

Help early users reach a result and decide to pay

People are arriving. Find out whether they can achieve the promised result. For an export tool, first value might be downloading a correct file. For an approval system, it could be receiving a real approval. An account measures registration; a useful first result measures activation.

Shorten the path to the first result

With permission, watch someone get started. Where do they stop? Must they import data before understanding the product? Do you ask them to invite a team too early? Does an error leave them unsure what to do? Let the session reveal these obstacles without directing every click.

You can provide a completed example, postpone unnecessary setup or assist the first use manually. Separate people reaching the result alone from those reaching it with help. Assisted success can demonstrate value while also revealing work still needed in onboarding.

Match trial and pricing to the way the product is used

A free trial helps when people can experience the result before buying. A demo may help when data, configuration or approval are needed. A free version can support distribution but still costs infrastructure and support. For a utility used once, a single payment may fit better than a subscription.

Make price, limits, renewal and cancellation clear. If someone gets value but does not buy, ask what is missing. They may no longer need it, lack purchasing authority or prefer an alternative. A discount does not automatically address those reasons.

Record events that mean something

For this example, define a relevant visit, completed account, first completed result and successful payment. Count users or accounts consistently, exclude internal tests and duplicates, and count a purchase only after actual payment confirmation. For asynchronous purchases, check the status provided by the payment system.

Visit → account → activation → customer is a teaching sequence. Change it if your product sells before use or has no accounts. Metrics should describe your own product.

On your project

Define an observable activation event and a sensible time window. Watch at least one first-use session and record the obstacles. Choose the first one to investigate, keeping assisted and independent outcomes separate.

07 / 09

Find out why people return or disappear

A payment is a concrete signal. Longer-term value becomes visible when the need returns. Daily products can show frequent returns; a quarterly tool needs the next relevant occasion. A utility purchased once behaves differently from a recurring service.

Measure a return to useful work

Define who enters the analysis, what counts as returning and the observation window. You might start at the first useful result and look for another result in a later period. A dashboard login or opened email may not represent that value. Amplitude’s retention documentation explains why starting and returning events need explicit definitions.

A cohort is a group sharing a starting point, such as users activated during the same week. Following groups separately prevents new arrivals from hiding the people who stop using the product.

Compare matching observation windows

In the diagram, cohort B has not reached its second week. The empty period is not zero, and you cannot compare it with cohort A’s week 2. Even 80% versus 60%, with ten people in each group, is a clue to investigate: audience, assisted use or usage occasion may have changed.

For subscriptions, distinguish customer churn from revenue loss. Two lost customers can have different effects if their plans differ. For one-time licenses, consider successful usage, refunds, support, additional purchases and referrals where relevant.

Use feedback to choose the next change

Ask people who leave what they wanted to achieve, where they stopped and how they solved the problem afterwards. Listen to people who stay too: they may rely on a part of the product you considered secondary. Investigate the need behind a feature request before deciding what to build.

Send useful messages at a relevant moment: instructions for completing a step, a requested answer or a meaningful update. Distinguish service messages from marketing and manage the applicable preferences. More email does not itself solve the problem causing people to leave.

On your project

Choose a return event and a time window matching the need. Prepare a cohort table and mark periods you cannot observe yet. Collect one reason for returning and one for leaving. Treat them as hypotheses to compare with the data.

Read the numbers and choose what to fix first

A dashboard helps when it changes a decision. Before increasing traffic, examine where the path stops and what it costs to help people through it. Use the same starting cohort, a defined observation window and consistent counts.

What you observeWhat to check first
Few relevant visitsDoes the channel reach the audience? Does the message make the problem recognizable?
Visits but few actionsAre offer, evidence, price and next step understandable? Does the path work?
Accounts but few first resultsWhich step prevents people reaching value?

The smallest percentage is not automatically the first problem to fix. A payment is a different commitment from a click. Look for an obstacle whose removal could improve the final outcome at reasonable cost. First check whether a missing event is causing the apparent problem.

With little traffic, answer questions you can investigate

With two purchases, one additional purchase changes the percentage substantially. Always show the underlying counts. An A/B test needs a sample appropriate to the starting conversion and effect you want to detect. No visit count works for every test. See the controlled-experiment guide by Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield.

Early on, observe real use, check comprehension of the offer and collect reasons for declining. Before-and-after comparisons can suggest a hypothesis but cannot isolate a cause if audience, channel or timing also change. Record those changes and the uncertainty.

On your project

Write a short report covering counts, conversions, costs, data quality and one obstacle to investigate. Propose an explanation and specify which observation would make you change your mind.

09 / 09

Repeat what works without exhausting your time or margins

Can you acquire more customers who receive value, at a sustainable cost? A successful launch or a satisfied customer is a starting point. Look for repeatability before multiplying channels, spending and features.

Check what remains of revenue

For each period, separate revenue, refunds, fees, infrastructure, usage-based services and support work. If the product uses paid APIs, observe actual usage cost and who generates the largest bills. Growing revenue can come with worsening margins.

In a teaching example, a €20 monthly subscription leaves €12 after the variable costs included in the calculation. If acquiring a customer costs €36, three months of that contribution recover the expense, provided the customer stays and costs and revenue remain similar. Taxes, fixed costs and any excluded work are not automatically accounted for.

With only a few months of history, lifetime-value estimates can be fragile. Start with what you have observed: contribution received, duration, churn, support and time to recover the acquisition expense.

Increase investment in steps you can check

Repeat a test on the channel that brought relevant customers. When increasing spending, check that quality, activation, margins and support still hold. Later audiences can behave differently. Automate repeated tasks you understand while keeping errors visible that personal contact previously revealed.

Consider a second channel for a concrete reason: reaching another compatible group, reducing dependence or reusing content that already helps. Choose work using expected impact, available evidence and effort. Avoid precise scores built from vague impressions.

Keep a decision log

For each change, record the hypothesis, audience, intervention, measure, limits and result. Close with a decision to continue, change or stop. Keep negative findings too: they help you avoid repeating a rejected idea without changing its conditions.

Research on hypothesis-based entrepreneurial decisions and experimentation in technology startups supports learning through tests. It does not establish this chapter order, a specific channel or a universal threshold as a guarantee of success. This guide offers a practical sequence to adapt to your project.

On your project

Choose your next test around the clearest remaining obstacle. Write down the investment you can sustain, what you will measure and when you will revisit the decision. Start again from the uncertainty still open, using what you learned.

Write your next test in full

Complete the worksheets for your project. With no users yet, start with a conversation about the problem. With visits but few useful first results, plan to observe actual use. Choose the test that answers your current uncertainty.

  1. Describe the audience, problem and evidence collected.
  2. Write the offer and commitment you want to observe.
  3. Prepare a page or demo and check the complete path.
  4. Define a channel, measure, time limit and spending cap.
  5. Record what happens and decide the next step, including what remains unknown.

The outcome is a reasoned decision. It might be to continue, narrow the audience, change the offer or stop investing in that direction.

Sources and limits of the method

These sources support specific concepts. Worksheets and chapter order are a teaching synthesis. No study certifies this sequence as a universal formula.

  • Y Combinator · How to Talk to Users

    Practical guidance on conversations about real incidents and behavior. The advice comes from operator experience; it has not been presented here as a controlled effectiveness study.

  • Camuffo and colleagues · Strategic Management Journal, 2024

    Four randomized trials involving 759 firms: effects on idea termination and strategic changes. This does not establish a universal marketing tactic.

  • Koning, Hasan and Chatterji · Management Science, 2022

    Empirical research on experimentation adoption in technology startups. Results depend on the study’s context and measures.

  • Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield · controlled experiments

    Methods for randomization and interpretation. No single minimum sample applies to every product.

  • Amplitude · retention analysis

    Technical documentation on starting and returning events. Retention benchmarks need additional context and evidence.

  • Google Analytics · campaign links

    Documentation of UTM parameters and campaign attribution. Choose measurement tools according to your own requirements.

  • Italian data protection authority · cookies and tracking

    An official Italian source for assessing measurement tools and consent requirements in the relevant context.
